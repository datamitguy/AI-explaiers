Chapter 1

What is a platform?

One word, two worlds, one first principle.

"Platform" means two related things. A platform business (App Store, Airbnb, Uber, YouTube) creates value by enabling interactions between outside producers and consumers. An internal platform (a company's developer platform) gives product teams self-service building blocks so they can ship faster. Both rest on the same first principle: a platform is a foundation that makes it dramatically cheaper for others to create value, and it succeeds only when those others choose to build on it.

That's why platforms are managed differently from ordinary products. Your success depends on an ecosystem you don't control. You can design incentives, rules and tools, but you can't order anyone to show up.

Every platform has a core interaction: the single most important exchange of value it enables, such as a stay booked, a ride taken, an app installed, or a service deployed. Design everything to make that interaction easier, more frequent and more valuable.

Chapter 2

Network effects & the cold start problem

A platform becomes more valuable as more people use it, which means it's nearly worthless when nobody does.

Network effects come in two kinds. Same-side effects: more players make a game more fun for players. Cross-side effects: more drivers make a ride app better for riders, and more riders make it better for drivers. They can also turn negative, through congestion, spam or low-quality supply. Economists Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole formalized these two-sided markets in 2003. Tirole went on to win the 2014 Nobel prize for his wider work on market power and regulation.

The flip side is the chicken-and-egg problem: riders won't come without drivers, and drivers won't come without riders. Andrew Chen's The Cold Start Problem (2021) describes the path: build an atomic network (the smallest network that works on its own, like one city or one campus), reach the tipping point, hit escape velocity, deal with the ceiling, and then defend the moat.

🏙️ Atomic network

Density beats size. Uber launched city by city, Facebook campus by campus. A small, dense network delivers value where a large, thin one doesn't.

💸 Subsidize the hard side

One side is usually harder to attract, often supply. Pay it, guarantee earnings, or waive fees, and let the other side pay.

🧰 Come for the tool, stay for the network

Chris Dixon's phrase. Give one side something useful on its own (Instagram's filters, OpenTable's reservation software), then add the network on top.

🌱 Seed & fake it (ethically)

Reddit's founders famously posted content themselves at first. Airbnb photographed listings for hosts. Early supply is often hand-built.

Chapter 3

Pricing: who pays, and how much?

On a platform, the side that pays is a strategic choice, not an accounting detail.

Two-sided pricing breaks ordinary intuition. It often pays to subsidize one side, even charging it nothing, because its presence makes the other side willing to pay more. Thomas Eisenmann, Geoffrey Parker and Marshall Van Alstyne's HBR article "Strategies for Two-Sided Markets" (2006) calls these the subsidy side and the money side. Consumers use search engines for free, while advertisers pay. Readers of PDFs paid nothing for Adobe's reader, while creators bought the authoring tools.

Then there's the take rate, the platform's cut of each transaction. Too low and the platform can't fund itself. Too high and supply leaves, prices rise, and participants start dealing directly with each other to avoid the fee. That's called disintermediation, or leakage.

Chapter 4

Governance, trust & winner-take-all

A platform is a small economy, and you write its laws.

Platforms govern their ecosystems. They decide who can join (curation), what's allowed (rules), how trust is built (ratings, verification, escrow), and how open to be (APIs, interoperability). Get it wrong and quality collapses: fake reviews, scams, spam. Negative network effects then drive good participants away. Increasingly, governments set rules too. The EU's Digital Markets Act (in force since 2022, with obligations applying from March 2024) forces designated "gatekeepers" to allow things like alternative app stores and interoperability.

Will this market be winner-take-all?

Network effects don't automatically crown a single winner. Many markets support several: riders and drivers happily multi-home across ride apps.

Chapter 5

AI is reshuffling the platform game

When coordination becomes cheap, whoever controls coordination gains power.

Sangeet Paul Choudary, co-author of Platform Revolution, argues in Reshuffle (2025, winner of the Thinkers50 Strategy Award) that AI's biggest effect isn't automating tasks faster. It's restructuring how work is coordinated across organizations and markets. Platforms have always been coordination machines, so AI changes who captures value. Tool makers become platforms, platforms bundle AI, and control shifts to whoever owns the system in which work gets coordinated.

🤖

Agents as a new "side"

AI agents that search, compare, book and buy on users' behalf become platform participants. Platforms must decide whether to welcome, charge, or block them.

🔌

Protocols & interoperability

Open standards like the Model Context Protocol (MCP) let AI systems plug into many services. Openness becomes a strategic lever again.

🧲

Aggregation of demand

AI assistants can become the new front door. If users ask an assistant instead of visiting a marketplace, the marketplace risks becoming a back-end supplier.

🏰

Envelopment

Platforms with overlapping user bases can absorb adjacent markets by bundling. AI features make bundling cheaper, so watch your adjacent platforms.

Chapter 6

Internal platforms: fighting cognitive load

Part B. The same first principle, turned inward: make it cheap for your own teams to create value.

Modern software teams are expected to handle cloud infrastructure, CI/CD, security, observability, data, compliance, and now AI tooling. That's more than any one team can hold in its head. Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais's Team Topologies (2019; 2nd edition, September 2025) makes team cognitive load the central design constraint. When a team is overloaded, flow slows and quality drops. Platform teams exist to absorb that load and offer it back as easy-to-use services.

Evan Bottcher's widely quoted definition (2018): "A digital platform is a foundation of self-service APIs, tools, services, knowledge and support which are arranged as a compelling internal product."

Chapter 7

Platform as a product

Your developers are customers who can choose not to use you. Act accordingly.

The core idea of modern platform engineering, developed in Team Topologies, in Camille Fournier and Ian Nowland's Platform Engineering (2025), and in Gregor Hohpe's Platform Strategy (2024), is to run the internal platform as a product. That means a product manager, user research with developers, a roadmap driven by their pain points, documentation, onboarding, and adoption that's earned rather than mandated. Start with the thinnest viable platform, sometimes just a wiki page of recommended practices, and grow only where there's demand.

The main tool is the golden path (Spotify's term) or paved road (Netflix's term): a well-supported, opinionated default way to do common tasks, such as creating a service, adding a database or deploying. Teams can leave the road, but staying on it is so much easier that most do.

🧹 Deprecation is a product skill

🗂️ Developer portals

A single front door (such as Backstage, open-sourced by Spotify and now a CNCF project) for the service catalog, templates, docs and ownership.

Chapter 8

Measuring a platform

If a platform is a product, its success is measured by its customers' success.

Three layers of research guide platform measurement. The DORA metrics (Accelerate, Forsgren, Humble and Kim, 2018, and the yearly DORA reports) track delivery performance: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to recover from a failed deployment. SPACE (2021) warns that productivity has many dimensions: satisfaction, performance, activity, communication and efficiency. DevEx (Noda, Storey, Forsgren and Greiler, 2023) focuses on what developers feel: feedback loops, cognitive load and flow state. Nicole Forsgren and Abi Noda's Frictionless (2025) turns this into a seven-step program for removing developer friction in the AI era.

Balance the three layers. Adoption (are teams choosing the golden path?), outcomes (DORA metrics for teams on and off the platform), and experience (developer satisfaction and friction surveys). A platform everyone is forced to use but secretly works around is failing, whatever the dashboards say.

Chapter 9

Platform maturity & the AI era

Platforms aren't built once. They mature through stages.

The CNCF's Platform Engineering Maturity Model (2023) assesses five aspects (investment, adoption, interfaces, operations and measurement) across four levels: provisional, operational, scalable and optimizing. It's a map of where you are, not a race to level four everywhere.

Platforms are the foundation for AI

The 2025 DORA report on AI-assisted software development surveyed nearly 5,000 technology professionals. It found that 90% of organizations have adopted at least one internal platform, 76% have a dedicated platform team, and there's a direct link between a high-quality internal platform and an organization's ability to get value from AI. The report's headline is that AI is an amplifier. With good platforms, clear workflows and strong engineering practices, AI speeds everything up. Without them, it produces "accelerated chaos". AI coding agents are also becoming platform users, so golden paths, templates and clear APIs make agents more effective too.

Quick check
Leadership wants to mandate the new internal platform for every team next quarter. What's the platform-as-a-product response?
Mandates hide product problems: teams comply on paper and work around the platform in practice. Voluntary adoption is the most honest signal that the platform actually reduces cognitive load.
Chapter 10

Domain platforms: who are my customers?

Part C. For leads of business-capability platforms (credit and lending, payments, customer data) who serve many teams and answer to many functions.

A domain platform (also called a business-capability platform) packages a business capability so that many products can reuse it. A credit and lending platform, for example, provides application intake, identity and bureau checks, decisioning, pricing, loan accounts, repayments and collections, so that the personal-loan, card, buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) and small-business lending teams don't each rebuild them. It sits between the two worlds of this course. It isn't a public marketplace, and it isn't only developer infrastructure. It carries business logic, regulatory obligations and money.

That's why the question "who is my customer?" feels impossible. Everyone seems to be one: the product teams, the underwriters, the risk team, compliance, finance, the borrowers. When everyone is your customer, you end up serving whoever shouts loudest. The way out is to separate the roles people play.

First principle: a customer is someone whose success you exist to create, and who chooses (or would choose, if they could) to use what you provide. A stakeholder is someone who constrains, funds or governs you. Both matter enormously, but you manage them differently. You earn a customer's adoption. You satisfy a stakeholder's constraints.

Customers are per capability, not per person. The credit risk team is a stakeholder of your decisioning API, because they set the policy it must enforce. They're also the customer of your rule-authoring and back-testing tool, because their job is to change policy quickly and safely. The same person can be your customer on Monday and your auditor on Tuesday, so name the capability before you name the customer.

Chapter 11

Drawing the platform boundary

What belongs in the platform, what belongs to the product teams, and what goes in between?

Most domain-platform conflicts are really boundary conflicts. The platform grows into a bottleneck that owns everyone's roadmap, or it shrinks into a thin API that every team works around. A practical rule of thumb for where things belong:

🏛️

Platform owns

Anything common across products, anything that must be controlled (regulated, audited, money-moving), and anything that changes slowly. Examples: the loan ledger, interest accrual, bureau integrations, decision records, adverse-action reasons and regulatory reporting.

🎛️

Self-service configuration

Anything that varies by product but must stay inside guardrails: pricing tables, product parameters, policy rules and document templates. Teams change these themselves, within limits the platform enforces.

🚀

Journey teams own

Anything that differentiates the product and changes fast: application screens, onboarding flows, marketing, channel-specific experiences and experiments.

Two ideas from software architecture help here. Chapter 12

Running a lending platform as a product

Roadmaps, metrics and operating rhythms, when every request seems urgent.

Write down each customer's job to be done

🚀 Journey team

"Launch a new loan product, or change an existing one, in weeks rather than quarters, without rebuilding decisioning, accounts or compliance."

📐 Credit risk team (as a customer of the rule tools)

"Change a cut-off or scorecard safely, see its impact on approval rates and losses before it goes live, and roll it back instantly."

🧑‍💼 Underwriter

"Decide a referred case quickly and consistently, with every piece of evidence on one screen."

📞 Collections agent

"Offer the right hardship option to a struggling borrower fairly and compliantly, first time."

🤝 Partner (lending-as-a-service)

"Embed credit in our product through a stable, well-documented API, and go live without a six-month integration."

🏠 Borrower (end customer)

"Get a fast, fair and understandable decision, and be treated well if I struggle to repay."

Measure customer outcomes, not platform activity

"Number of APIs" and "tickets closed" measure activity. Measure whether your customers are succeeding, and keep the guardrails visible.

Prioritize for leverage, with deadlines as fixed constraints

A domain platform's backlog mixes product requests, regulatory deadlines, operational pain and technical debt. There are two first principles. Regulatory obligations with fixed dates are constraints, not options: schedule them first and protect their capacity. After that, prefer leverage, meaning work that makes many consumers faster or safer. A request that only one team needs usually belongs in that team's backlog, or should be made possible through self-service configuration.

Operating model & anti-patterns

✅ Healthy patterns

• A platform product manager paired with a tech lead and a risk/compliance partner embedded in the team.
• A monthly customer council of journey-team leads to review the roadmap together.
• Team Topologies modes: X-as-a-Service with journey teams by default, time-boxed collaboration when launching a new product type, and facilitating to teach the risk team the self-service tools.
• Compliance by design: decision records, reasons and audit trails built into the platform, so every product gets them free.
• Versioned APIs, published service levels, and planned deprecations.

🚫 Anti-patterns

• The service desk: everything arrives as a ticket, and the platform becomes the bottleneck for every product launch.
• Loudest stakeholder wins: the roadmap follows escalations, not leverage.
• Platform takeover: the platform starts owning journey teams' screens and product roadmaps.
• Gold-plating: building for hypothetical future products instead of real current consumers.
• Mandated adoption: teams comply on paper and build shadow systems.
• Forgetting the borrower: optimizing internal convenience while decision times or fairness get worse.

Lending is regulated, and decisions about people's credit are high stakes. Examples include adverse-action notices under the US Equal Credit Opportunity Act (Regulation B), the EU AI Act's classification of creditworthiness assessment as high-risk, and the UK FCA's Consumer Duty (2023), which requires firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers. Treat these as product requirements the platform delivers once, well, for everyone. That's one of the strongest reasons a lending platform exists at all.

Quick check
The SME lending team, the credit risk team and compliance all demand to be "the priority customer" of your lending platform. What's the first-principles answer?
Name the capability first, then its customers. Borrowers are the ultimate "why", and internal teams are how you serve them at scale. Compliance's requirements are non-negotiable constraints, not a competing wish list.
Chapter 13 · Part D: Making the case

Why a central Power Platform team for credit?

You lead a credit platform built on Microsoft Power Platform. Development today is fragmented and federated. This part gives you the narrative, the numbers and the labs to make the case to senior management, and the slides to take away.

First, say what a platform is in one sentence

"A digital platform is a foundation of self-service APIs, tools, services, knowledge and support which are arranged as a compelling internal product." That's Evan Bottcher's 2018 definition, and it's still the clearest. For a senior audience, simplify it to: a platform is a shared set of building blocks, rules and services, run as a product, that makes it faster, cheaper and safer for many teams to build what the business needs. The platform doesn't build every app. It makes every app easier, safer and cheaper to build.

For a Power Platform estate in credit, that means: the environments, connectors to core credit systems, a shared credit data model, reusable components, entitlements, guardrails, pipelines, support and know-how that every team uses, so that the teams who know the business (credit risk, underwriting, portfolio management, operations) can deliver apps and automations quickly without each one reinventing the plumbing or carrying the risk alone.

The story in four lines (SCQA)

Executives remember a story, not a list. Barbara Minto's Situation, Complication, Question, Answer structure (from The Pyramid Principle) is the standard way consultants frame a recommendation. Here is a draft you can adapt:

📍 Situation

Power Platform has become how credit gets things done. Dozens of teams have built apps and flows close to the business: credit applications, covenant tracking, limit reviews, watchlists, reporting. It delivered speed.

⚠️ Complication

Because every team builds alone, we now pay for it: the same capabilities rebuilt many times, inconsistent credit data, access granted app by app, uneven controls on apps that touch credit decisions, key-person risk, licence waste, and no safe foundation for AI and Copilot.

❓ Question

How do we keep the speed and business ownership of federated development, while gaining the scale, consistency and control of a platform?

✅ Answer

Create a small central Credit Power Platform team, run as a product, that centralises the commons and federates the journeys: one paved road to production, shared building blocks, one data model, one entitlement model and risk-based guardrails, while business-aligned teams keep building.

The symptoms of fragmentation (do you recognise these?)

🔁

Duplication

Five versions of "approval workflow", three customer search screens, several connectors to the same loan system, each built and maintained separately.

🧩

Inconsistent data

Exposure, limits and ratings copied into SharePoint lists and Excel, with different definitions, no lineage and no single source of truth.

🔐

Access sprawl

Every app has its own roles and its own access process. Nobody can answer "who can approve what, across all our apps?" quickly, and leavers keep access.

🧯

Uneven control

Some apps go through testing, review and change control; others are edited live in production by one person. Audit findings arrive one app at a time.

🧑‍🔧

Key-person risk

Critical apps are understood by one maker. When they move on, the app becomes a black box, and nobody dares change it.

💸

Hidden cost

Licences bought per team and under-used, premium capacity in the wrong places, environment sprawl, and the same skills hired several times.

None of this means federated development was a mistake. It is the normal life cycle: low-code spreads because it works, and then the organisation needs a platform to make that spread sustainable. Your message isn't "teams did it wrong". It's "we've succeeded enough that it's time to industrialise".

Chapter 15

Central, federated, or hub-and-spoke?

"Bring it all together" doesn't have to mean "a central team builds everything". It usually shouldn't.

The biggest risk in your pitch is that senior management hears "centralise" and imagines a new bottleneck, or that business teams hear it and fear losing control. The model most large organisations converge on for low-code is hub-and-spoke: a central platform team (the hub, sometimes called a Center of Excellence) owns the shared foundation and guardrails, while fusion teams in the business (makers, business analysts and professional developers working together) keep building the apps. Microsoft's own Power Platform guidance describes this pattern, and it matches Team Topologies: the hub is a platform team plus an enabling team; the spokes are stream-aligned teams.

Chapter 16

Entitlements as a platform service

Platform-wide entitlements are not a side topic. In credit, "who can see and approve what" is one of the strongest reasons to have a platform at all.

In a fragmented estate, each app invents its own roles, stores them in its own table or SharePoint list, and grants access by email request. The result: role explosion, slow joiners, leavers who keep access, and no way to prove segregation of duties (SoD), for example that the person who prepares a credit application can't also approve it. A platform-wide entitlement model fixes this once:

1. Personas
Define business personas once: relationship manager, credit analyst, approver (by authority level), credit risk, operations, auditor, maker, platform admin.
2. Groups
Map each persona to Microsoft Entra ID security groups, fed by HR joiner/mover/leaver events where possible.
3. Roles
Map groups to standard Dataverse security roles, teams and business units (plus field-level security for sensitive data) used by every app.
4. Policies
Encode SoD rules and delegated authority limits (who can approve what amount) as shared data and checks, not code in each app.
5. Evidence
Run periodic access recertification and keep audit logs centrally, so audit gets one answer for all apps.

For the deck: "Today we can't answer 'who can approve credit across our apps?' without asking each app owner. With a platform entitlement model, it's one query, access changes when people change jobs, and segregation of duties is enforced by design." That one sentence often lands better with risk-minded executives than any efficiency number.

Chapter 17

Lego blocks, data & design

Reuse is where a platform turns into speed. But blocks only get reused if they're easy to find, trustworthy and better than building your own.

The "Lego blocks" for a credit Power Platform come in five kinds: connectors (custom connectors and APIs to the loan origination system, core banking, credit bureaus, rating models, collateral and document stores), components (Power Apps component libraries and code components built with the Power Apps component framework (PCF)), flow patterns (approval, escalation, notification, document generation), templates (starter apps and solutions with the right security and logging already in place), and data (shared Dataverse tables for the credit data model). Each is a small product with an owner, documentation, versioning and a support promise.

Chapter 18

Governance that speeds people up

The best governance is proportionate to risk and built into the paved road, so the safe way is also the easiest way.

One-size-fits-all governance fails both ways: a simple team tracker drowns in paperwork, while a spreadsheet-replacing app that feeds credit decisions slips through as "just a low-code app". Banks usually treat such apps as end-user computing (EUC) or end-user developed applications, with controls set by how critical they are. Risk-tiering lets you go light where risk is low and strict where it's high.

The guardrails the platform provides

🌍 Environment strategy
  • Dev, test and production environments per solution or domain, not one shared default
  • The default environment kept for personal productivity, with tight data policies
  • Managed Environments and environment groups to apply rules at scale
  • Self-service environment requests with automatic setup
🚦 Data loss prevention
  • Data policies that group connectors into business, non-business and blocked
  • Stricter policies for production and sensitive data
  • Custom connectors approved and published centrally
  • Tenant isolation to control cross-tenant connections
🔁 ALM (application life cycle management)
  • Everything in solutions, with environment variables and connection references
  • Source control and automated deployment (Power Platform pipelines, or Azure DevOps or GitHub with the Power Platform Build Tools)
  • Solution checker, code review and automated tests for higher tiers
  • No direct editing in production
🔍 Visibility
  • An inventory of every app, flow, maker, connector and environment (for example, the CoE Starter Kit)
  • Ownership and orphaned-app clean-up when makers leave
  • Usage telemetry to find duplicates and retire unused apps
  • Audit logs into the bank's security monitoring
🧯 Resilience
  • Criticality assessment for apps that support important business services
  • Backup, restore and business continuity plans for tier 1 apps
  • Monitoring and alerting on failed flows
  • Operational-resilience and third-party-risk evidence
🤖 AI guardrails
  • Rules for Copilot Studio agents and AI Builder: data access, human review and testing
  • No AI-only credit decisions; human in the loop and explainability
  • Prompt and agent catalogue with owners
  • Alignment with model-risk policy where outputs inform decisions

Regulation is a reason for a platform, not a reason for paperwork. Credit is subject to rules on fair lending, consumer outcomes, operational resilience, data protection and model risk. Examples include the UK FCA's Consumer Duty, the EU AI Act (which treats creditworthiness assessment of individuals as high-risk), the EU Digital Operational Resilience Act and model-risk guidance such as the US Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 and the UK PRA's SS1/23. When the platform builds audit trails, access control, change control and evidence into the paved road, every app inherits compliance instead of each team re-solving it. Check which rules apply to your bank with your risk and compliance partners.

Who: a small team with the right mix

🧭 Platform product owner

Owns the vision, roadmap, intake and value story. Treats makers and business teams as customers. (Probably you.)

🏗️ Platform architect

Environment strategy, integration patterns, data model, security design, standards.

⚙️ Platform engineers / admins

Tenant and environment administration, pipelines, monitoring, DLP, licensing, automation of the platform itself.

🧑‍💻 Pro developers

Custom connectors, PCF components, Azure integration, complex reusable blocks.

🗄️ Data engineer / modeller

Credit data model, data products, integration with systems of record, Power BI semantic models.

🎨 UX designer

Design system, credit UX patterns, accessibility, user research with business teams.

🎓 Enablement lead

Maker training paths, champions network, office hours, documentation and community.

🛡️ Risk & control partner

Embedded from risk/compliance: risk tiering, EUC policy alignment, evidence and audit liaison.

Start smaller than this if you need to (some roles part-time or shared), but cover product ownership, architecture, engineering and enablement from day one. A platform team with no enablement becomes a gatekeeper; one with no product owner becomes a ticket queue.

Five rules for a senior-management deck

🎯

Ask first

Put the decision you want on slide 2: "We're asking for approval to set up a central Credit Power Platform team of N people, funded by X, starting in Q?".

📰

Action titles

Every slide title is a full sentence that states the point ("Fragmentation costs us about £X a year"), not a label ("Costs"). Read only the titles and the story should still make sense.

1️⃣

One message per slide

If a slide needs two messages, it's two slides. Put the detail in an appendix.

🧾

Evidence they trust

Use your own estate data (app counts, duplicate builds, audit findings, access requests) over industry statistics. One real example beats ten bullet points.

🛡️

Pre-empt the objections

Know the five questions they'll ask and answer them in the deck or the appendix. Meet the key stakeholders one-to-one before the meeting.
