The bias & fallacy codex

200 ways the mind takes shortcuts, 40 ways arguments go wrong, and how to catch both.

A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern of error in judgement. Most come from heuristics: mental shortcuts that are usually useful and occasionally misfire. Buster Benson (2016) grouped the famous list of biases by the four problems they help us solve: too much information, not enough meaning, the need to act fast, and what to remember. This page follows that map in eight sections of 25, then adds two sections on logical fallacies: errors in the structure of an argument, rather than in perception or memory.

Each compact card gives the bias, why it happens, how to counter it, an example and an evidence note. Psychology's replication crisis hit this field hard, so the rating matters: some "biases" are rock solid, some are smaller than first claimed, and a few are folk terms or statistical fallacies rather than quirks of the mind. Biases with a full deep-dive elsewhere on the site link to it (↗).

Knowing about biases doesn't make you immune: the bias blind spot means we spot them in others far more easily than in ourselves. What helps is process: checklists, base rates, pre-mortems, outside views, devil's advocates and decisions made before emotions run hot.

Part I · 25 biases

👀 What grabs our attention

Problem 1: too much information. The brain filters hard, so we notice what is vivid, repeated, recent, surprising, negative or about us, and we judge things by contrast rather than on their own.

Part II · 25 biases

🔒 Seeing what we already believe

Also too much information: the brain's easiest filter is "does this fit what I already think?". These biases protect our beliefs, our choices and our sense of being reasonable.

Part III · 25 biases

🎲 Patterns, probability & statistics

Problem 2: not enough meaning. We fill gaps with patterns and stories, and our intuitions about chance, samples and probability are systematically off.

Part IV · 25 biases

👥 Judging people & groups

Also not enough meaning: with little information about others, we fill in with stereotypes, first impressions, our own perspective and assumptions about their character.

Part V · 25 biases

🪞 Overconfidence & the self

Problem 3: the need to act fast. To act at all we need confidence, so we overrate our skill, knowledge, control and future, and protect our self-image.

Part VI · 25 biases

⚖️ Decisions, risk & value

Also the need to act fast: we lean toward the status quo, the certain, the simple and whatever we already own or invested in, and we judge value relative to reference points.

Part VII · 25 biases

🐑 Social influence & groups

Acting fast in a social species means following others: authority, the crowd, reciprocity and group harmony all steer what we believe and do.

Part VIII · 25 biases

🧠 What we remember

Problem 4: what should we remember? Memory is reconstructive: we edit, generalise and distort the past, and some things stick far better than others.

Part IX · 20 biases

🥊 Fallacies of relevance

Logical fallacies part 1: arguments that attack the person, dodge the point or lean on authority, popularity or emotion instead of reasons. Common in meetings, politics and comment threads.

Part X · 20 biases

🧩 Fallacies of structure, cause & evidence

Logical fallacies part 2: invalid forms, false choices, slippery slopes, confusing correlation with causation, and moving the goalposts.

A debiasing toolkit

You can't switch biases off. You can design around them.

📊 Start with the base rateBefore the vivid story, ask how often this kind of thing happens in general (the outside view).
🔍 Seek disconfirmationAsk "what would prove me wrong?" and look for it. Assign someone to argue the other side.
🧊 Decide coldMake important choices before the heat of the moment: rules, checklists and pre-commitments beat willpower.
🔄 ReframeFlip gains to losses, percentages to frequencies, "keep" to "buy": if your answer changes, the frame was deciding.
⏳ Imagine the future and the failureRun a pre-mortem, and widen your estimates: we're usually too narrow and too optimistic.
🗳️ Collect views independentlyAsk people for their judgement before they hear others', to avoid anchoring, cascades and groupthink.
📝 Write it downRecord predictions and reasons, so hindsight and memory can't rewrite them later.
🤝 Be kind about itBiases are features of every human mind. Point them out in processes, not in people.
