Chapter 1

What is AI, really?

"Artificial intelligence" is an umbrella term. Underneath it sit several nested ideas, each more specific than the last.

Artificial intelligence is any technique that lets a computer do something we'd normally say takes intelligence: recognizing a face, translating a sentence, planning a route, writing a poem.

For decades, most AI was hand-written rules: "if the email contains 'FREE MONEY', mark it as spam." That works until the spammer writes "FR3E M0NEY". The big shift in modern AI is that we stopped writing the rules and started letting machines learn them from examples.

Three levels of capability

People also sort AI by how general it is. Every AI system in use today, however impressive, is some kind of narrow AI: it's good at a set of tasks, not everything a human can do. Today's large language models are the broadest narrow AIs ever built, which is why the line has started to feel blurry.

Spectrum
We are here
Narrow AI (ANI)General AI (AGI)Superintelligence (ASI)
🎯

Narrow AI

Excels at specific tasks: chess engines, spam filters, image recognition, today's chatbots. It exists today.

🧠

General AI

Could learn and do almost any intellectual task a person can, and transfer skills between them. Hypothetical, and heavily debated.

🚀

Superintelligence

Would exceed the best humans across nearly every domain. Speculative, and the main focus of long-term AI safety research.

Quick check
A system follows 5,000 hand-written "if/then" rules to diagnose car engine faults. Which category fits best?
It counts as AI (an "expert system", popular in the 1980s), but nothing is learned from data. A human wrote every rule, so it isn't machine learning.
Chapter 2

How machines learn

Machine learning comes in three main flavors. What separates them is the kind of feedback the machine gets.

Live simulation
Three ways to learn
🏷️

Supervised

Learn from labeled examples ("this is spam", "this is not"). Used for classification and prediction.

🧩

Unsupervised

Find structure in unlabeled data: clusters, patterns, compressions. Used for customer segmentation and anomaly detection.

🎮

Reinforcement

Learn by trial and error from rewards. It's how AlphaGo mastered Go, and part of how chatbots learn to be helpful.

🔮

Self-supervised

A clever mix: hide part of the data and predict it. "Predict the next word" is how LLMs learn from raw text without human labels.

Learning = rolling downhill

Nearly all modern ML works the same way underneath. Define a loss, a number that says how wrong the model is. Then nudge the model's settings in whatever direction lowers the loss, and repeat. This is called gradient descent: a ball rolling down a hill in the dark, feeling for the slope.

Interactive
Gradient descent
Click anywhere on the curve to drop the ball. Then change the learning rate, the size of each step. Too small and it crawls. Too big and it overshoots.

Notice that the ball can get stuck in the shallower right-hand valley, a local minimum, even though a deeper one exists to the left. Real training uses tricks like momentum and noisy mini-batches to escape, and in spaces with billions of dimensions, true dead ends turn out to be rare.

Training a model with billions of parameters is the same idea as this ball, just in a landscape with billions of dimensions instead of one.

Quick check
You set the learning rate very high and the loss starts jumping around wildly. What's happening?
Big steps overshoot the bottom and land on the opposite slope, sometimes higher than before. Picking a good learning rate (and changing it during training) is a big part of the craft.
Chapter 3

Neural networks

Neural networks are loosely inspired by the brain. Their basic unit, the artificial neuron, is surprisingly simple.

A neuron takes a few numbers in and multiplies each by a weight (how much it matters). It adds them up with a bias, then squashes the result into a range like 0 to 1. That's the whole thing. Learning means finding good weights.

Interactive
A single neuron: "Should I go to the beach?"
Set the inputs (what's true today) and the weights (how much you care about each one). Line thickness shows weight size. Rose lines are negative weights.
Output = σ(Σ w·x + b)0.00

Stack them up: deep learning

Wire thousands of neurons into layers, so that each layer feeds the next, and you get a neural network. The early layers learn simple features (edges, letter shapes). Deeper layers combine those into complex concepts (faces, meanings). "Deep" learning just means many layers.

The algorithm that trains them, backpropagation, works out how much each weight contributed to the error and nudges every one of them downhill at once. It's gradient descent across the whole network.

In the beach neuron, you set the weight on "It's raining" to a large negative number. What does that do?

Chapter 4

Data & feature engineering

A model is only as good as the data it learns from. In real projects, most of the work happens before any training starts.

Practitioners often say that 80% of machine learning is data work. Before a model can learn anything, someone has to collect the data, clean it, label it, split it, and turn raw columns into useful signals. Mistakes here don't cause errors. They quietly produce a model that looks fine in testing and fails in the real world. That's why "garbage in, garbage out" is the oldest rule in the field.

What is a feature?

A feature is one measurable input the model uses to make a prediction, such as the square footage of a house, the hour a transaction happened, or the number of words in a review. Feature engineering means creating better inputs from raw data, using what you know about the problem. A good feature can turn a hard problem into an easy one, as the next demo shows.

Interactive

Adding x² and y² lets the model compute x² + y², which is the squared distance from the center. A straight line in that new feature space becomes a circle in the original one. Deep neural networks learn transformations like this automatically ("representation learning"), which is part of why they're so powerful. For tabular business data, hand-crafted features still often win.

Data leakage is the most dangerous feature mistake. It happens when a feature contains information that wouldn't be available at prediction time, like a "refund_issued" column when you're trying to predict which orders will be returned. The model looks brilliant in testing and fails in production. Always ask: would I actually know this value at the moment I need the prediction?

In larger organizations, engineered features are kept in a feature store, a shared catalog that computes each feature the same way for training and for live predictions. That consistency matters. A feature computed slightly differently in production than in training ("training–serving skew") is one of the most common reasons deployed models underperform.

Quick check
You're predicting whether a loan applicant will default. Which feature is data leakage?
Missed payments happen after the loan is approved, so you can't know them when you make the decision. The feature would make the model look amazing in testing and be useless in real life.
Chapter 5

Training & evaluating models

How do you know if a model is actually good? Not by testing it on the data it studied.

A model that has seen the answers can simply memorize them, just as a student who has seen the exam questions can ace the test without understanding anything. So we split the data before training. The model learns from the training set. We tune settings on the validation set. And we hold back a test set that is touched only once, at the very end, to estimate real-world performance.

Underfitting vs. overfitting

A model that's too simple underfits: it misses real patterns. A model that's too flexible overfits: it memorizes the noise in the training data and fails on new data. The goal is the sweet spot in between, and the only way to find it is to measure error on data the model hasn't seen.

Interactive
Fit a curve, and watch the test error
Filled dots are training data and hollow dots are unseen test data. Raise the model complexity (the polynomial degree). Training error always falls, but test error falls and then shoots back up.
↔ swipe the diagram to see all of it

Fixes for overfitting include more data, simpler models, regularization (penalizing overly complex fits), and early stopping. Cross-validation, which rotates which slice of the data is held out, gives a more reliable estimate when data is scarce.

Accuracy isn't everything

Imagine a fraud detector that says "not fraud" to every transaction. If 99% of transactions are legitimate, it's 99% accurate and completely useless. That's why we look at the confusion matrix, which breaks predictions into four kinds of right and wrong, and at metrics built from it.

Interactive
Threshold, precision & recall
The model gives each email a spam score from 0 to 1. Anything above the threshold goes to the spam folder. Slide it, and watch the trade-off between catching spam (recall) and not trashing real mail (precision).
↔ swipe the diagram to see all of it

Precision: of the emails flagged as spam, how many really were spam? Recall: of all the spam, how much did we catch? F1 balances the two. For cancer screening, you'd favor recall (don't miss cases). For a spam folder, you'd favor precision (don't lose real mail).

Quick check
Your model scores 99% on the training set but 71% on the test set. What's the most likely problem?
A big gap between training and test performance is the classic sign of overfitting. Try more data, regularization, or a simpler model, and keep validating on held-out data.
Chapter 6

Deployment & model monitoring

Training a model is the beginning, not the end. The world keeps changing after launch, and models quietly get worse.

MLOps (machine learning operations) is the discipline of running models reliably in production. It borrows from software engineering (versioning, testing, CI/CD) and adds problems specific to ML. Your "code" now includes data and learned weights, and a model can fail silently while every server looks healthy.

Explore
The ML lifecycle is a loop
Tap any stage. Monitoring feeds back into data collection and retraining. Production ML is never "done".

Ways to deploy

🌐

Real-time API

The model sits behind an endpoint and answers each request in milliseconds, for example fraud checks at checkout.

🗓️

Batch

Score millions of rows overnight, for example tomorrow's churn-risk list. It's simpler and cheaper when you don't need instant answers.

📱

On-device / edge

Runs on phones, cameras and cars. It's private and works offline, but the model must be small.

🐤

Safe rollouts

Shadow mode runs the new model silently alongside the old one. Canary releases send it 5% of traffic first. A/B tests compare the two, and a model registry makes rollback one click.

Model monitoring

Models go stale because the world drifts away from the training data. Data drift means the inputs change: new customer segments, a new app version, seasonality. Concept drift means the relationship between inputs and outcome changes: fraudsters adapt, tastes shift, a pandemic rewrites shopping habits. Monitoring catches both before your users do.

📦 Data quality

Missing values, schema changes, out-of-range inputs. These are the cheapest problems to catch and the most common.

📊 Drift

Compare live input distributions with training data using statistics such as PSI or the KS test.

🎯 Performance

Accuracy, precision and recall once true labels arrive. That often takes days or weeks, so drift is an early warning.

⚙️ Operations

Latency, error rates, throughput and cost per prediction: the usual service health metrics.

Quick check
Input distributions look identical to training, but your fraud model's accuracy is sliding week after week. What's the likely cause?
When inputs look the same but outcomes change, the relationship has shifted. That's concept drift. The fix is fresh labeled data and retraining.

Quick check
Your k-nearest-neighbors model with k = 1 scores 100% on the training set but 74% on the test set. What's the best next move?
With k = 1, every training point is its own nearest neighbor, so training accuracy is always 100%. It's memorization. A larger k averages over neighbors and usually generalizes better. Never report training accuracy as your result, and never train on your test set.

The paper that started it all

Before 2017, language models read text one word at a time using recurrent networks (RNNs and LSTMs). That was slow, because it couldn't run in parallel, and forgetful, because information from early words faded by the end of long sentences.

NeurIPS 2017 · Google Brain & Google Research
"Attention Is All You Need"
Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan N. Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser, Illia Polosukhin
"We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely."

💡 The insight

Let every word look directly at every other word at the same time, using self-attention. There's no recurrence and no fading memory.

⚡ Why it mattered

It runs fully in parallel on GPUs, so you can train much bigger models on much more data.

🌱 What it spawned

BERT, GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, and image, audio and protein models too. The "T" in GPT stands for Transformer.

The paper was originally about translating English to German and French. Nobody knew yet that the same architecture, scaled up, would learn to write code, pass exams and hold conversations. That discovery came from the scaling laws (2020): the loss of language models falls predictably as you add parameters, data and compute.

Quick check
What was the key limitation of RNNs that the Transformer removed?
Sequential processing meant no parallelism and a weakening signal over long distances. Attention connects any two positions in a single step.
Chapter 9

Inside a transformer

A large language model does one thing: predict the next piece of text. Here's how it gets from your words to that prediction.

Step 1: Text becomes tokens

Models don't see letters or words. They see tokens: common chunks of text, each with an ID number. Common words are usually one token. Rare words get split into pieces. The "·" marks a leading space, since spaces are part of tokens. Token counts matter because pricing and context limits are measured in tokens, not words. A rule of thumb for English is about ¾ of a word per token.

Step 2: Tokens become meaning-vectors

Each token is turned into an embedding: a long list of numbers (thousands of them) that places the token in a "meaning space". Similar meanings end up near each other. Directions in that space can even encode relationships. Nobody places these points by hand: the vectors are learned during training, so words used in similar contexts drift close together.

A 2-D sketch of a space that really has thousands of dimensions. Pick an analogy and watch vector math find the answer.

Step 3: Attention, where words look at each other

Here's the transformer's superpower. For every token, self-attention asks: "Which other tokens should I pay attention to, to understand what I mean here?" The word "it" can't be understood alone. It needs to find what it refers to.

Interactive
Watch attention resolve "it"
Tap any word to see which earlier words it attends to. (In a decoder model like Claude, each word can only look back.) Compare "it" with the final word, then swap the final word and watch what it links back to.

Under the hood, each token produces a query ("what am I looking for?"), every token offers a key ("what do I contain?"), and well-matched query–key pairs decide how much of each token's value gets mixed in. A real model runs dozens of these attention heads in parallel, each learning to track different things (grammar, references, facts, tone), and stacks dozens of layers of them.

Step 4: Predict the next token

At the top, the model outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary. It picks one, appends it to the text, and runs again, one token at a time. The temperature setting controls how adventurous that pick is. Technically, temperature divides the model's raw scores before they become probabilities: below 1 sharpens the favorite, and above 1 flattens the field.

Interactive
Temperature & sampling
Low temperature makes the model predictable. High temperature makes it creative, and eventually chaotic. Sample a few times at each setting.
The cat sat on the ___

An LLM is a next-token predictor, trained on a huge amount of text. To predict the next word of a physics textbook, a legal brief or a Python program well, it has to learn a lot of physics, law and Python along the way.

Chapter 11

Beyond text: diffusion

Image and video generators mostly use a different trick: they learn to turn noise into pictures.

Diffusion models (a key 2020 paper was "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" by Ho, Jain and Abbeel) are trained by taking real images and adding noise step by step until only static is left. The model learns to undo one small step of noise. To generate something, you start from pure static and denoise it hundreds of times, guided by your text prompt.

Interactive
From noise to image
Drag the timestep. t = 1000 is pure noise and t = 0 is the clean image. (Our demo cheats and already knows the answer. A real model has to predict the noise at each step.)

Signal kept: 0%. Early steps settle the big layout. Late steps add fine detail.

How does the prompt come in? The model is trained on millions of image–caption pairs, and at every denoising step the text embedding steers which noise to remove, so the picture drifts toward something that matches your words. Video models do the same across many frames at once.

Other generative families include GANs (2014), where two networks compete, a forger against a detective, and VAEs (2013), which compress data and decode it again. Many new video and image models combine diffusion with transformer backbones.

Prompt engineering

Prompting, RAG, fine-tuning or agents?

These techniques solve different problems, and you can stack them. Prompting shapes behavior. RAG adds knowledge. Tools and agents let the model act. Fine-tuning bakes in a specialized style or skill. A common mistake is fine-tuning to add facts, which is usually better done with RAG.

Evals: unit tests for AI

LLM outputs vary, so "it looked good when I tried it" isn't a test. Teams build eval suites: sets of realistic inputs, each with a check. The check might be a rule, a reference answer, or an LLM-as-judge grading against a rubric. Every prompt or model change is run against the suite, which catches regressions (things that used to work and now don't) before users see them.

Chapter 15

The frontier: RAG, JEPA, Jev & beyond

What comes after "a bigger transformer"? Here are the ideas pushing AI forward.

RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation

LLMs only know what was in their training data, and they can hallucinate confidently. RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) fixes this by looking things up first. Your question is used to search a knowledge base, the best-matching passages are pasted into the prompt, and the model answers grounded in those sources, with citations.

RAG is how most companies connect LLMs to their own documents. Real systems use neural embedding models and vector databases instead of our keyword scoring, often combined with re-ranking. The idea is the same.

JEPA: predicting meaning, not pixels

Yann LeCun (Meta) argues that predicting every word or pixel is wasteful, because the world is full of unpredictable detail, like the exact shape of every leaf. His proposal is the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), set out in the 2022 position paper "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence". A JEPA predicts the abstract representation of the missing part instead of the raw pixels. Models such as I-JEPA (2023), V-JEPA (2024) and V-JEPA 2 (2025) put this into practice, with the goal of building world models that understand physics and can plan.

Jev: System One models

Most of this course has been about models that generate text, pixels or tokens. In September 2026, TypeSafe AI, a San Francisco startup founded in 2024, took a different approach with Jev, launched in early access on 15 September 2026. Jev generates no text at all. You give it some input (the "state") and a set of typed questions. It returns an answer to every question with a calibrated probability, all in a single parallel pass.

TypeSafe calls this a System One model, after Daniel Kahneman's split between fast, intuitive System 1 thinking and slow, deliberate System 2 reasoning. LLMs that think step by step are System 2. Jev aims to be the fast, reflexive System 1: a judgment that software can call like any other function.

⚡

System 1 · Jev

Fast, structured, reflexive decisions.

  • Output is typed answers, not prose
  • All questions answered in one parallel pass
  • About 70 to 500 ms end to end (most around 100 ms), as reported by TypeSafe
  • Probabilities trained to match reality
🧠

System 2 · LLMs like Claude

Slow, deliberate, open-ended reasoning.

  • Output is free-form text, code and plans
  • Generated token by token
  • Can think at length about hard problems
  • Good for writing, coding and multi-step analysis

Why "calibrated" matters

If a model says 0.9 on a hundred inputs, about ninety of them should really be true. That's what calibrated means, and it lets software set thresholds: auto-approve above 0.95, send to a human below 0.6. TypeSafe says Jev is transformer-based, trained entirely on synthetic data using Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), which optimizes probabilities against real outcomes rather than human preference ratings.

✅

Where it fits

Ticket and email routing, content moderation, resume screening, scoring, verification, and agent guardrails. It can also choose which model or how much thinking a request needs. TypeSafe has even shown it making split-second choices in games like Doom.

⚠️

Where it doesn't

It doesn't write articles or code or answer open-ended questions. TypeSafe's documented limitations include exact counting, math and date comparison, deep multi-step reasoning, over-literal reading of instructions, and "context rot" when given lots of irrelevant input.

The emerging pattern is hybrid. A System One model like Jev handles the fast, high-volume decisions (classify, route, score, guard), and a System Two LLM like Claude is called only when open-ended reasoning or generation is actually needed. TypeSafe reports pricing of $0.042 per million input tokens, with output free, and says Jev is up to 200× faster and 400× cheaper than comparable LLMs on classification tasks. These are the company's own figures, and it's still early access.

Sources: TypeSafe AI: "Introducing System One Models & Jev" · TypeSafe docs · MarkTechPost · The Register

Quick check
Which job is the best fit for a System One model like Jev?
It's a high-volume, typed decision where calibrated confidence lets you auto-act or escalate. Long-form writing belongs to a generative LLM, and TypeSafe lists date arithmetic as a known weakness. That's a job for ordinary code.

Mixture of Experts (MoE)

Why run a whole giant model for every token? In an MoE, each layer has many "expert" sub-networks and a small router that sends each token to just a few of them. You get the knowledge of a huge model at the compute cost of a much smaller one. Many recent open-weights frontier models use it. For example, a model might hold several hundred billion parameters in total but activate only a few tens of billions for each token.

In practice experts don't split into neat human topics like "math" or "French". Their specializations are learned and often hard to interpret.

Test-time compute: thinking longer

One of the biggest recent shifts: instead of only making models bigger, let them spend more computation while answering, by reasoning, checking, and trying alternatives. On hard math and coding tasks, accuracy keeps climbing as the thinking budget grows, though with diminishing returns.

A company wants its chatbot to answer from its 10,000-page internal wiki, which changes weekly. What's the best first approach?

Chapter 16

Responsible AI

Fairness, transparency, security and regulation: building AI that deserves people's trust.

AI systems now help decide who gets a loan, a job interview or a medical follow-up. Models learn from historical data, and history contains bias, so a model can reproduce or even amplify unfairness without anyone intending it. Responsible AI means measuring these effects, explaining decisions, and keeping humans accountable.

There are several mathematical definitions of fairness: equal approval rates, equal error rates, equal opportunity. Researchers have shown that, in general, you can't satisfy all of them at once. Choosing between them is a values decision, not just a technical one. That's why fairness needs domain experts, affected communities, and often regulators, as well as engineers.

Regulation: the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024 and applies in stages, regulates AI by risk level. The higher the potential harm, the stricter the rules. Other regions are building their own frameworks, but the risk-based idea is spreading.

Other risks to design for

💉

Prompt injection

Hidden instructions in a web page or email ("ignore previous instructions and…") can hijack an AI agent. Treat all retrieved content as untrusted data, and limit what agents are allowed to do.

🌫️

Hallucination

Confident but false outputs. Use grounding (RAG), citations, evals, and human review for high-stakes answers.

🔒

Privacy

Personal data in training sets and prompts. Minimize what you collect, redact it, set retention limits, and get consent.

🎭

Misinformation & deepfakes

Synthetic media at scale. Watermarking, provenance standards (like C2PA) and disclosure rules are the main responses.

⚡

Environmental cost

Training and serving large models uses significant energy and water. Efficiency gains and smaller models help.

🧭

Human oversight

Keep a human in the loop for consequential decisions. Document models with model cards, and red-team before launch.

Final check
A hiring model doesn't use race as a feature. Can it still discriminate by race?
Removing a sensitive attribute ("fairness through unawareness") rarely works, because correlated features leak the same information. You have to measure outcomes across groups to know whether a model is fair.
