Chapter 1

What product management actually is

Strip away the frameworks and the job titles, and one idea is left.

Here is the first principle: a product exists to create value for customers in a way that also creates value for the business. Everything a product manager does serves one of those two halves, or the tension between them. Tickets, roadmaps and standups are tools. The job is making sure the team builds something that is wanted, usable, buildable and worth it.

Marty Cagan (Inspired, Empowered, Transformed) frames this as four risks every product idea carries. A strong product team tackles them before committing engineers to build, because most ideas fail, and the cheapest place to discover that is in discovery, not after launch.

💎

Value risk

Will customers buy it, or choose to use it? This is the risk that most often kills products.

🧭

Usability risk

Can people figure out how to use it and get the value?

🛠️

Feasibility risk

Can we build it with the time, skills, technology and data we have?

🏛️

Viability risk

Does it work for the business: sales, legal, finance, brand, compliance?

Outputs, outcomes, impact

Melissa Perri's Escaping the Build Trap named the most common failure mode: teams that measure success by what they ship (outputs) rather than what changes for customers (outcomes) and for the business (impact). Shipping is a cost. Outcomes are the point.

Interactive
Output, outcome or impact?

Outputs are things we make. Outcomes are changes in customer behavior. Impact is the business result. Give teams outcomes to own, not a list of outputs to deliver.

Chapter 2

Start from the customer's problem

People don't want your product. They want progress in their lives.

Clayton Christensen's Jobs to Be Done theory (Competing Against Luck, 2016) says customers "hire" products to make progress in a particular circumstance. In the famous milkshake study, morning commuters were hiring a milkshake to make a long, boring drive more bearable and to stay full until lunch. Their real competition wasn't other milkshakes. It was bananas, bagels and boredom. Bob Moesta's Demand-Side Sales 101 extends the idea: map the forces that push someone to switch (frustration with today, the pull of something better) and the ones that hold them back (anxiety, habit).

Talk to customers the right way

Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test starts from the fact that people lie to be nice, especially about your idea. So don't ask what they would do. Ask about what they did. Talk about their life, not your idea. Ask for specifics from the past, not opinions about the future. Listen far more than you talk.

Continuous discovery and the opportunity solution tree

Teresa Torres's Continuous Discovery Habits (2021) turned discovery from a phase into a weekly habit. A product trio (a PM, a designer and an engineer) talks to customers every week and maps what they learn in an opportunity solution tree. The tree runs from a desired outcome, through the customer opportunities (needs, pain points, desires) that could drive it, to candidate solutions, and finally to the small assumption tests that check them.

Compare and contrast. The tree forces you to consider several opportunities and several solutions per opportunity, instead of falling in love with the first idea. The "whether or not" decision becomes a "which one" decision.

Chapter 3

Strategy: choosing what not to do

A strategy is a set of choices that makes some things easier and others impossible. Anything else is a wish list.

Richard Rumelt (Good Strategy / Bad Strategy, 2011; The Crux, 2022) argues that most corporate "strategies" are really goals dressed up as plans, or fluff. Real strategy has a kernel of three parts: a diagnosis of what's going on, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent actions that carry out the policy and reinforce each other.

🎯 Playing to Win

A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin (2013) describe a cascade of five linked choices: winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, the capabilities you'll need, and the management systems that support them. The two middle choices are the heart of it.

📍 Positioning

April Dunford's Obviously Awesome (2019) says positioning is context. Start from the customer's competitive alternatives, then identify your unique attributes, the value they enable, the customers who care most, and the market category that makes that value obvious.

🏰 7 Powers

Hamilton Helmer (2016): lasting advantage comes from scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, a cornered resource or process power. If you can't name your power, you don't have a moat yet.

🔭 Vision → strategy → roadmap

Vision says where you're going (years out). Strategy is the few bets that get you there. The roadmap sequences problems to solve, ideally as now / next / later rather than a date-driven feature list.

Chapter 4

Prioritization: betting under uncertainty

You will always have more good ideas than capacity. Prioritization is an economic decision.

Two first principles apply. The first is that value per unit of effort matters more than value alone. The second is that confidence should be earned by evidence, not by who argues loudest. Intercom's RICE score captures both: Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Donald Reinertsen (The Principles of Product Development Flow) adds the cost of delay. Every week an item waits has a price, so dividing cost of delay by duration (CD3) tells you what to do first.

The evidence ladder is adapted from Itamar Gilad's Confidence Meter (Evidence-Guided, 2023). The opinions of senior people and your own conviction count for very little. Customer evidence, experiment results and launch data count for much more. The point isn't precise numbers but honesty. A big idea backed only by conviction shouldn't outrank a medium idea with test results behind it.

Chapter 5

Experiments: learn cheaply, decide honestly

The goal of discovery is not to prove you're right. It's to find out fast and cheaply when you're wrong.

Every idea rests on assumptions. Map them by importance (would the idea fail if this is wrong?) and evidence (how much do we actually know?). Test the important, unproven ones first, with the cheapest test that could change your mind: a fake-door button, a concierge or Wizard-of-Oz service run by humans behind the scenes, a clickable prototype, and only then an A/B test on real traffic.

A/B tests and the peeking problem

Online controlled experiments are the gold standard for measuring impact, and they are easy to get wrong. Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu's Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020) catalogs the traps. One of the most common is peeking: checking results every day and stopping as soon as they look "significant". Run the simulation below. Every test is an A/A test, where both versions are identical, so any "win" is a false alarm.

Twyman's law: any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong. Decide the sample size and success metric in advance, include guardrail metrics (latency, revenue, complaints), and be suspicious of wins that are too good to be true.

Chapter 6

Metrics that matter

What you measure is what you'll get, so measure the value customers receive, not just the activity.

A North Star metric (popularized by Amplitude's North Star Playbook) captures the core value customers get, and leads to business results. Examples are nights booked for Airbnb or messages sent within teams for Slack. Beneath it sit a few input metrics that teams can actually move, and around it sit guardrails that must not get worse.

Retention: the truest signal of value

Acquisition can be bought, but retention can't. Plot the share of a sign-up cohort still active each week. If the curve keeps sliding toward zero, the product isn't delivering lasting value yet. If it flattens, some group of customers has found something they keep coming back for, which is one of the strongest signs of product–market fit. Sean Ellis's survey offers another test: if at least 40% of users would be "very disappointed" without your product, you're probably there.

Chapter 7

Teams and the product operating model

How you organize decides what you can build.

Cagan's Empowered (2020) contrasts feature teams, which are handed features to build, with empowered product teams, which are given problems to solve and held accountable for outcomes. Transformed (2024) describes the wider shift to a product operating model: change how you decide what to build (product strategy and discovery), how you build (small, frequent, reliable releases), and how you solve problems (cross-functional teams owning outcomes). Melissa Perri and Denise Tilles's Product Operations (2023) covers the supporting layer of data, customer insights and processes that lets many teams make good decisions at scale.

🎯 OKRs

Christina Wodtke's Radical Focus (2nd ed., 2021): a few objectives, measurable key results expressed as outcomes, and a weekly rhythm to keep them alive, not quarterly theater.

🗺️ Story mapping

Jeff Patton's User Story Mapping (2014): lay out the user's journey, then slice thin releases through it, so each release is a whole, usable experience.

🧑‍🏫 Coaching

Petra Wille's Strong Product People (2021): product skill is built through deliberate coaching and practice, not job titles.

💰 Pricing

Madhavan Ramanujam's Monetizing Innovation (2016): design the product around the price, and talk willingness to pay early, before you build.

Chapter 8

Product management in the AI era

The first principles haven't changed. The speed, the materials and the risks have.

Three things are different now. First, building got cheap. AI coding agents can turn an idea into a working prototype in hours, so the bottleneck moves even further toward knowing what's worth building. Discovery matters more, not less. Second, AI features are probabilistic: the same input can produce different outputs, and some of them will be wrong. That makes evals, test suites of realistic cases with clear pass criteria, a core product artifact, as important as the spec. Third, AI amplifies the organization you already have. Google's 2025 DORA research, based on nearly 5,000 technology professionals, found that AI makes strong teams stronger and makes struggling teams' problems worse.

🧪

Evals are the new spec

Define "good" with examples and graders before building. Every prompt or model change runs against the suite, and production failures become new test cases.

⚡

Prototype to learn

Use AI to build throwaway prototypes in discovery, and test them with real customers the same week. SVPG's "INSPIRED in the Generative AI Era" argues this raises the bar for discovery rather than removing it.

💸

New unit economics

Every AI call has a cost and a latency. Model choice, caching and routing are product decisions with margin consequences.

🛡️

Trust & safety by design

Plan for wrong answers: show sources, allow easy correction, keep a human in the loop for consequential actions, and watch for prompt injection.

For a practitioner's view of this shift, see Henrik Werdelin and Nicholas Thorne's Me, My Customer, and AI (2025), on staying obsessively close to customers while AI collapses the cost of building. For the engineering side, see Chip Huyen's AI Engineering (2025).

Quick check
AI agents let your team ship features three times faster. What's the biggest risk?
Building was never the scarcest resource. Knowing what's valuable was. Faster delivery without better discovery produces more outputs, not more outcomes. That's the build trap at higher speed.
